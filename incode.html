Incode Fan ID

Designing identity verification at national scale — 770,000 fans, 17 stadiums, one consistent flow.

Context

A security failure that demanded a design solution.

In March 2022, a violent incident at a Liga MX match between Atlas and Querétaro exposed a systemic failure: stadiums had no reliable way of knowing who was entering. Sports authorities responded by mandating identity verification across all national stadiums. Incode was selected to build the solution. I led product design working directly with PM Poojit Sharma.

These results came from a single consistent onboarding flow, designed to work for 770,000 people across different ages, devices, and digital literacy levels — from tech-savvy users to first-time smartphone users.

The Problem

Manual checks don't scale.

The challenge wasn't only technical. Tens of thousands of fans had to complete biometric verification before entering — many unaware the requirement existed, with varying levels of digital literacy, under time pressure before kickoff. A slow or confusing flow wasn't just a UX problem: it was a real security bottleneck.

Design Decision 01

Shift friction before the gate.

The core principle: move verification pressure away from the entry point. By encouraging pre-event registration, gate congestion dropped significantly. For the verification flow itself, I separated ID capture and face capture into distinct guided steps — reducing cognitive load and error rates at each stage.

Design Decision 02

Status-first hierarchy for operators.

The operator dashboard had to support fast decision-making under real event-day pressure. I structured information in a status-first hierarchy — critical verification states immediately visible, evidence organized to support review without hesitation. Exception handling was designed to resolve edge cases without disrupting access control flow.

Design Decision 03

Turning verification into a reusable layer.

Beyond single-event access, the identity wallet extended verification into a persistent layer — linking tickets, credentials, and future access points under one verified profile. One registration, any stadium, any match.

The brief

The request came in on a single afternoon, triggered by a security incident at a Liga MX match. It went to Poojit Sharma first, and the brief was blunt: 'We need a system where the fan can verify their identity, and FMF can confirm they are who they say they are.' No spec, no wireframe reference — just the problem, stated in one sentence, with two weeks to build it.

First attempt, first mistake

My first instinct was to collect as much user data as possible during onboarding — the more we knew, the safer the system, or so I assumed. It was wrong. Beyond adding real time and stress to the user's onboarding, most of what we collected was never used and got discarded later. This became the first real lesson of the project: more data is not more security.

The constraint

Two weeks meant external identity verification was severely limited — there wasn't time to integrate deep third-party checks. Poojit's direct line to FMF (Mexico's Football Federation) became the project's lifeline: he fed ideation and information in real time as I designed against a moving brief.

The onboarding flow that shipped.

Field research, after beta

Once the beta was live, research shifted from assumption to evidence: on-the-ground observation of user behavior, friction points, onboarding time, and drop-off — all pulled from the operator dashboard backend and heatmap data. This is where the real signal started.

Stripping down

The research showed onboarding took too long because of that extra data collection — friction and motivation loss, later confirmed against marketing's engagement metrics. But fixing it wasn't linear. It was like building a tower out of Legos: technically the structure held with just the basics, but without enough body, it broke for edge cases. One version removed all tutorials entirely, betting users would find the flow intuitively — the curse of knowledge: because I understood it, I assumed everyone would. That version failed. There were several other variants tested along the way that didn't survive.

Simplifying the dashboard

The dashboard I inherited surfaced every signal from every onboarding report at once. More information didn't mean more clarity — for live verification decisions, it meant the opposite. Working with Poojit, I restructured it around a single principle: for real-time verification, less is more.

The rhythm with Poojit

Iteration was constant — Poojit was my calibration point throughout. We'd revisit the onboarding flow, test different ways to make it more direct, and rarely disagreed hard enough to need resolving; we worked in enough sync that we consistently landed on the same conclusions.

I also built Incode's functional motion practice from scratch during this period — see the motion work.

Getting the brand right

FMF didn't have a clear point of view on branding. Early color choices clashed with existing football club identities; when FMF proposed their own, the palettes lacked coherence. After roughly three rounds of revision, we landed on Incode's own brand colors from that period — the pragmatic choice that actually worked.

Field execution

Two problems surfaced only once we were live, neither of them a design problem. The first was bandwidth: stadium entry points couldn't handle verification traffic, resolved by IT running dedicated internet lines directly to the entrances to speed up assisted, on-site registration. The second was onboarding completion: we split registration into two parts — the essential minimum required to verify identity, and a second, non-critical layer of information. Marketing built an incentive program around completing the second part, which worked, but only temporarily, and mainly with the 16–40 age group, per marketing's data.

What I actually owned

To be specific about scope: I directly designed the fan-facing web app, the onboarding flow, and the FanID wallet — including its extra layer for face-based payments inside stadiums and linked services. The operator dashboard was Incode's existing product; my contribution there was collaborating with Poojit to simplify it for live event use, not designing it from the ground up.
